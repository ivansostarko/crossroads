Step 1 · The situation

What decision are you facing?

Writing the situation down in plain words is the first act of deciding. Research on expressive writing shows that naming a problem reduces its emotional noise and makes it easier to reason about.

Is this decision reversible?

Jeff Bezos calls these one-way vs. two-way doors. Reversible decisions deserve speed; irreversible ones deserve care.

Step 2 · Widen your options

What could you do?

The single biggest decision mistake found in research (Nutt, 1993; Heath & Heath, 2013) is narrow framing — treating a choice as "whether or not" instead of "which of these". Add at least two real alternatives.

Vanishing options test

Imagine your current options disappeared. What would you do then? If something comes to mind — add it above.

Step 3 · Weigh the good and the bad

Pros & cons — with weight

In 1772 Benjamin Franklin described his "moral algebra": list reasons for and against, then strike out items of equal weight until one side clearly wins. Modern decision analysis keeps the core idea — not every reason counts equally. Rate each from 1 (barely matters) to 5 (life-shaping).

Step 4 · Reality-test your thinking

Check yourself before you decide

Kahneman and Tversky showed that predictable biases quietly steer our choices. Ticking through them doesn't remove them, but naming a bias measurably weakens its pull.

Step 5 · Prepare to be wrong

The premortem

Gary Klein's technique, endorsed by Kahneman as his favourite debiasing tool: imagine it is one year from now, you chose your leading option, and it turned out badly. Prospective hindsight increases the ability to identify reasons for failure by ~30%.

Set a tripwire

Decisions drift. Pick a date or a signal that forces you to re-evaluate instead of coasting on autopilot ("If I haven't seen X by [date], I will reconsider").

Step 6 · The verdict

Where the weight falls

The scale below adds up your weighted pros and cons. It is a mirror, not an oracle — if the result surprises or annoys you, that feeling is information too (the "coin-flip test").
